About

A Career at the
Edge of Power

Phillip L.B. Halpern's federal career began not with a landmark case but with a phone call. In November 1984 — weeks after joining the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of California — he took a duty call from a customs inspector at the Tecate Port of Entry who had detained a smuggler carrying unfamiliar medications. The DEA said they weren't controlled substances. Should he release him? When the inspector read the labels over the phone, Halpern recognized them immediately: anabolic steroids. Within three years, he had indicted 34 defendants in some of the nation's first major steroid prosecutions — a ring that controlled an estimated 70 percent of the illegal steroid black market and moved $2 to $4 million in product annually.

That case set the template for what followed. For 36 years, Halpern pursued the cases other offices found too complex, too politically sensitive, or too novel to prosecute. He indicted an international network smuggling F-14 fighter jet components to Iran — parts stolen directly from U.S. Navy carriers and maintenance facilities. He prosecuted the first violation of the Reagan administration's Libya embargo. He convicted a pharmacist who had jumped bail, fled to London, and was running a counterfeit prescription drug operation before being caught in an undercover sting — sentenced to 14 years. He indicted co-conspirators in a scheme to smuggle injectable sheep-placenta extract and cow-brain extract into the United States as "fountain of youth" substances touted by Sylvester Stallone.

The cases that defined his later career were the ones that defined an era. The investigation into Duke Cunningham began with a newspaper story about a suspiciously inflated real estate transaction. Within weeks, Halpern's team had executed search warrants on Cunningham's home, his defense contractor's office, and the "Duke-Stir" — a yacht the contractor had moored in Washington D.C. for the Congressman to live on, renamed in his honor. Cunningham pleaded guilty five months later. The Duncan Hunter investigation began similarly — another Union-Tribune tip, another search, this time of a sitting Congressman's Capitol Hill office, one of the most rarely executed actions in federal law enforcement. Hunter fought the charges for over a year before his wife agreed to testify against him.

Since leaving federal service, Halpern has written and spoken widely on DOJ independence, prosecutorial ethics, and the weaponization of law enforcement for political ends. His commentary in the San Diego Union-Tribune reflects the thinking of a lawyer who spent four decades not theorizing about justice — but delivering it.

"Our very democracy is at risk when criminals win elections by weaponizing the tropes of fake news and the deep state."

— Government's Sentencing Memorandum, United States v. Duncan D. Hunter (2020)

Cases & Experience

Notable Federal Prosecutions

The cases that defined a career — and, in several instances, reshaped American political history. Each prosecution tested a single enduring principle: that no one in the United States is above the law.

01

United States v. Randy "Duke" Cunningham

Bribery · Public Corruption · U.S. Congress

The investigation began with a newspaper story about a suspiciously inflated real estate transaction. Within weeks, search warrants had been executed on Cunningham's home, his defense contractor's office, and the "Duke-Stir" — a yacht the contractor had moored in Washington D.C. for the Congressman to live on, renamed in his honor. What emerged was one of the largest congressional bribery cases in American history: more than $2.4 million in bribes in the form of cash, a Rolls-Royce, antiques, and home improvements — a corruption so brazen it required its own price list, which prosecutors dubbed the "bribe menu."

Cunningham pleaded guilty five months after the first newspaper story appeared, wept on the steps of the courthouse, and resigned from Congress. Sentenced to over eight years in federal prison — the longest sentence ever imposed on a sitting member of Congress at that time — the case was a reminder that public adulation is no shield from accountability.

Co-lead prosecutor with AUSAs Jason Forge, Sanjay Bhandari, and Valerie Chu.

02

United States v. Duncan Hunter

Campaign Finance Fraud · Wire Fraud · U.S. Congress

Congressman Duncan Hunter and his wife Margaret plundered $250,000 in campaign funds donated by citizens to finance luxury vacations in Italy and Las Vegas, groceries, video games, school tuition, and a family trip to watch the Pittsburgh Steelers from a private box. They overdrew their personal account more than a thousand times in seven years, racking up $38,000 in overdraft fees, and subsidized their lifestyle entirely through theft from their own supporters.

What made this prosecution historically significant was Hunter's response. Rather than accept responsibility, he weaponized the "deep state" narrative — attacking the FBI, the DOJ, and prosecutors themselves — and used that false conspiracy to win re-election in 2018, returning a soon-to-be-convicted felon to Congress. The government's sentencing memorandum argued presciently that democracy itself is at risk when criminals win elections by weaponizing fake news and the deep state. Hunter pleaded guilty and was sentenced to federal prison.

Co-lead prosecutor with AUSAs Emily Allen and Mark Conover.

03

United States v. Kyle "Dusty" Foggo, et al.

Fraud · Conspiracy · CIA Executive Director

Kyle "Dusty" Foggo rose to become the Executive Director of the Central Intelligence Agency — the third-highest position in the entire intelligence community and the official responsible for its day-to-day operations — before a federal investigation dismantled his career. The investigation required executing a search warrant inside CIA headquarters, in Foggo's own office — believed to be among the most extraordinary law enforcement actions ever taken against a sitting senior intelligence official.

Connected to the same web of defense contractor corruption that ensnared Duke Cunningham, Foggo was convicted of fraud for steering CIA contracts to a longtime friend in exchange for personal benefits. He remains, to this day, the highest-ranking intelligence official ever indicted in the United States — a distinction that speaks to both the audacity of the prosecution and the reach of the corruption it exposed.

Co-lead prosecutor with AUSAs Jason Forge and Valerie Chu.

04

United States v. Gregory Marino ("Operation Bullpen")

Fraud · Forgery · Largest Sports Memorabilia Conspiracy in U.S. History

Operation Bullpen unspooled like a collector's dream turned forensic nightmare. Centered in Southern California, the scheme flooded the market with forged autographs of Babe Ruth, Mickey Mantle, and Ted Williams — thousands of fake baseballs, jerseys, and photographs sold through dealers, auction houses, and seemingly legitimate authentication channels. Skilled forgers produced convincing signatures in bulk while conspirators created bogus certificates of authenticity, allowing fakes to command premium prices from unsuspecting collectors who believed they were acquiring a piece of history.

When investigators peeled back the layers, they uncovered a nationwide conspiracy involving dozens of participants and millions of dollars in fraudulent sales spanning the entire memorabilia industry. Marino pleaded guilty, and numerous co-conspirators were convicted. The case remains a landmark in sports fraud enforcement — exposing how fragile the trust-based economy of collectibles becomes when authenticity itself is the product being counterfeited.

Sole prosecutor.

05

United States v. Floyd Landis

Wire Fraud · Cycling · Tour de France

This prosecution was a postscript to one of cycling's most dramatic rises and falls. After being stripped of his 2006 Tour de France title for doping, Landis launched a high-profile defense effort — soliciting hundreds of thousands of dollars from loyal supporters with assurances he would use the funds to prove his innocence and clear his name. The government alleged those representations were false: donor money was diverted to personal expenses, turning what began as a public relations crusade into a wire fraud case built on misrepresentation and the systematic betrayal of trust.

Landis ultimately pleaded guilty. The case carried a certain dark irony: a man once undone by substances coursing invisibly through his bloodstream was later convicted over money moving just as quietly through electronic wires. His eventual admission of doping helped crack open one of the most elaborate performance-enhancement conspiracies in professional sport, implicating Lance Armstrong and triggering an international reckoning that reshaped cycling.

Sole prosecutor.

06

United States v. Saied Asefi Inanlou, et al.

Halpern with F-14 gimbal assembly seized during the investigation, c. 1985

Sole prosecutor.

National Security · Arms Smuggling · Iran

After the fall of the Shah, Iran's revolutionary regime faced a critical vulnerability: its air force — the most potent in the Middle East — depended on American F-14 Tomcats, and spare parts had dried up with the severance of U.S.-Iran relations. The solution was audacious: recruit Navy sailors and civilian employees to steal components — including gimbal assemblies for the F-14 inertial navigation system and missile guidance parts — directly from aircraft carriers, maintenance facilities, and supply depots in California, Virginia, and the Philippines.

Running from 1981 to 1985, stolen components were shipped through front companies, mislabeled as auto parts or medical supplies, routed through New York and London, and delivered to Iran. The investigation uncovered dozens of shipments worth millions of dollars across a network of up to 20 co-conspirators. Key figures received sentences of 13 to 18 years. The case sparked a congressional investigation and led to lasting reforms in how the Navy safeguards sensitive military components.

07

United States v. Maureen O'Connor

Tax Fraud · Charitable Foundation · Former Mayor of San Diego

Maureen O'Connor served two terms as Mayor of San Diego and was, by most accounts, one of the city's most beloved public figures. What unfolded in the decade ending in 2009 was a story of addiction, desperation, and the betrayal of public trust on a staggering scale. O'Connor reported over $1 billion in gambling winnings to the IRS — and lost even more. To cover her mounting debts and continue gambling, she misappropriated approximately $2.1 million from the charitable foundation established by her late husband, Bob Peterson, the founder of Jack in the Box.

To conceal the embezzlement and avoid taxes on the funds, she characterized the transfers as loans. In one of the rare federal criminal prosecutions involving the abuse of a charitable organization, O'Connor admitted her misconduct, agreed to repay the foundation in full, and resolved all resulting tax liabilities. The case was a stark reminder that public admiration, however well-earned, confers no immunity under the law.

Sole prosecutor.

08

United States v. Jose Susumo Azano-Matsura, et al.

Foreign Election Interference · Campaign Finance · First of Its Kind

Azano, a wealthy Mexican defense contractor, was convicted of injecting nearly $600,000 in illegal foreign money into San Diego's 2012 mayoral race — to benefit candidates Bob Filner and Bonnie Dumanis — despite a clear prohibition on foreign nationals funding U.S. campaigns. Working through political operatives, straw donors, and a former police detective, Azano built a shadow campaign apparatus while concealing the true source of the funds.

The motive was ambition and ego: Azano sought access to advance "Miami West" — a lavish San Diego Bay redevelopment worth hundreds of millions to him. Evidence included wiretaps, emails warning co-conspirators not to put anything incriminating in writing, and testimony about cash-filled envelopes. Convicted on 36 felony counts, he was sentenced to three years in federal prison and fined over $560,000. A warning that American elections are not for sale — even to someone with a mansion in Coronado and a checkbook the size of a small nation's GDP. How quaint that warning seems today.

Supervising prosecutor, assisting AUSAs Andrew Schopler, Mark Pletcher, Helen Hong, and Billy Joe McLean.

09

United States v. Patrick Frega, et al.

Judicial Corruption · RICO · California Superior Court Judges

The corruption in the Frega case unfolded not in a back alley but inside San Diego's own courthouse. Over more than a decade, attorney Patrick Frega showered three sitting Superior Court judges with more than $100,000 in benefits — car repairs, cash, ghostwritten novels, vacations, and payments to relatives — in exchange for favorable treatment in dozens of high-stakes civil cases worth millions.

The most sensational testimony came from Judge Greer — the former presiding judge — who cooperated and admitted accepting roughly $75,000 in bribes while driving a Mercedes provided by Frega and, by his own account, feeling physically sick about his corruption. Because charging state court judges with federal crimes presented novel legal obstacles, a RICO indictment was crafted premised on state-law bribery, framing the scheme as theft of the public's right to honest services from its judiciary. All three judges were convicted — exposing a courthouse culture where favors and friendships quietly determined six-figure verdicts.

Co-lead prosecutor with AUSAs Charles LaBella and Tom McNamara, assisted by AUSAs Kevin Kelly and John Kirby.

10

United States v. Hilma Marie Witte, et al.

Murder · Conspiracy · Family as a Criminal Enterprise

Most criminal conspiracies are held together by money or greed. The Witte case was held together by something far more disturbing: a mother's authority over her own children. Hilma Marie Witte, an Indiana housewife, was charged with orchestrating the murders of her husband and mother-in-law by recruiting her own children to carry out the killings — turning maternal trust into the engine of the crime.

The facts were extraordinary. The mother-in-law was killed with a crossbow. Her remains were dismembered and scattered in the family garden until police grew suspicious — at which point what was left was placed in a Coleman cooler, transported across the country in the family RV, and dumped in the Otay Mesa landfill in San Diego. What made the case especially chilling was not just the violence but the inversion of every protective role: the person expected to nurture became the architect of harm, using family trust as both shield and weapon.

Sole prosecutor.

11

United States v. Robert Morales

Bribery · Money Laundering · IRS Agent

The largest bribery case ever brought against an IRS Agent began with a simple premise: the man assigned to police the nation's tax laws had quietly converted his position into a private toll booth. Over roughly a decade, Morales accepted more than $600,000 in bribes from a wealthy taxpayer in exchange for shielding him from audits and helping conceal millions in income from the government.

The scheme was anything but crude. Morales and his son constructed a maze of sham corporations, ghost employees, and phony payroll checks, laundering payments through relatives while living far beyond his reported IRS salary — cars, investments, Super Bowl tickets. The investigation culminated in an undercover sting in which Morales agreed to launder supposed drug money, sealing his fate. He pleaded guilty to conspiracy, bribery, money laundering, and tax fraud, and was sentenced to 12 years in federal prison — the tax man undone by his own ledger of corruption.

Sole prosecutor.

12

United States v. Mario Saikhon

Tax Evasion · Bribery · Largest Individual IRS Prosecution in History

Mario Saikhon orchestrated a tax shelter fantasy that kept adding rooms until it collapsed under its own weight. A wealthy Imperial Valley farmer, Saikhon spent years concealing millions in income by partnering with a corrupt IRS agent — the same agent at the center of the Morales prosecution — paying hundreds of thousands in bribes in exchange for audit protection and inside guidance on staying invisible to the government.

Rather than relying on a single scheme, Saikhon constructed a sprawling paper architecture of sham corporations, nominee accounts, and fictitious payroll arrangements that siphoned off profits while making his taxable income appear modest. The relationship evolved into something closer to a long-running criminal enterprise than a one-off payoff. When investigators finally followed the money, Saikhon was convicted on multiple felony counts including conspiracy, tax evasion, and bribery — the largest individual criminal tax prosecution in the history of the Internal Revenue Service.

Lead prosecutor, assisted by DOJ Trial Attorney Steven Ward.

13

United States v. Robert Bradley, et al.

Tax Fraud · Charitable Foundation Abuse · First of Its Kind

The Bradley case broke new ground in federal tax enforcement: it was the first criminal tax prosecution ever brought against a charitable foundation in the United States. Bradley and his co-conspirators had constructed what appeared to be a legitimate charitable entity but was in reality a vehicle for financial fraud — exploiting the tax-exempt status and public goodwill that society extends to charitable organizations to conceal conduct that was anything but charitable.

The prosecution required navigating novel legal terrain: no federal criminal tax case had previously targeted a charitable foundation as the instrument of the offense. The case added a critical new chapter to the government's toolkit for holding abusers of the charitable form accountable, and established that the nonprofit designation provides no immunity from criminal tax enforcement when a foundation is used as a personal piggy bank.

United States v. A&W Sales Corp., et al.

Corporate Accountability · Food Safety · Public Health

When a Hepatitis A outbreak spread through grade schools across the Midwest, federal investigators traced the source to a food contractor that had cut corners in its production and distribution processes. The prosecution of A&W Sales Corp. held a corporate defendant directly accountable for the human cost of food safety failures — children sickened, families disrupted, a community's trust in the school lunch program shaken.

The case made clear that federal criminal enforcement is not confined to financial fraud but reaches wherever corporate misconduct causes direct and measurable harm to real people. Securing a conviction against a food contractor for conduct that sickened schoolchildren required building a chain of evidence from the contaminated product back through the company's production practices — and making the argument that cutting corners on safety is not merely a regulatory failure but a federal crime.

15

United States v. Javid Naghdi

Counterfeit Prescription Drugs · Largest FDA Case in History

Naghdi was a pharmacist operating an international counterfeit prescription drug ring of staggering scale — the largest such case ever prosecuted by the Food and Drug Administration. After being indicted and posting bail, he fled to London, where he continued running the operation from overseas, apparently calculating that distance from the United States would put him beyond reach. He was wrong.

Working with British authorities, investigators tracked Naghdi through an undercover sting operation that brought him back within the reach of federal law. He was apprehended, returned to the United States, and sentenced to 14 years in federal prison. The case stands as a landmark in FDA criminal enforcement — demonstrating that fugitives who attempt to run sophisticated drug counterfeiting operations from abroad will eventually be found, and that the sentence at the end of the road will reflect the full scope of the misconduct.

16

United States v. Metabolife International, et al.

Corporate Fraud · Consumer Safety · Dietary Supplements

At its peak, Metabolife International was the dominant company in the American dietary supplement industry, generating hundreds of millions in annual revenue from ephedra-based weight-loss products. The federal case exposed a systematic pattern of concealment: the company had received reports of serious adverse health events — heart attacks, strokes, seizures — linked to its products and failed to disclose them to the FDA as required by federal law, choosing revenue over the health of its customers.

The prosecution contributed to the eventual regulatory ban on ephedra-containing supplements and established an important precedent for corporate accountability in an industry that had long operated in a regulatory gray zone. The case demonstrated that the loosely regulated supplement market was not beyond the reach of federal criminal enforcement — and that concealing known safety risks from the government is a crime, not merely a business decision.

17

United States v. Richard Anthony Fitton

Drug Smuggling · First Federal Steroid Prosecution

Before anabolic steroids were classified as controlled substances under federal law, prosecuting their distribution required creative legal thinking and an intimate knowledge of how sophisticated smugglers operated. Fitton ran an international steroid trafficking network that concealed product in tire wells, false compartments, and fake gas tanks to move inventory across the U.S.–Mexico border. Halpern pursued him for several years — long enough to assemble three separate federal charges — until Fitton ultimately pled guilty.

The charges — smuggling under 18 U.S.C. § 545, unlawful introduction of a prescription drug in interstate commerce, and failure to declare illegal income — were assembled from existing statutes in the absence of steroid-specific criminal law. The case became the first federal prosecution of steroid distribution in the country's history, establishing the legal framework that would govern all subsequent enforcement and earning Halpern a reputation, as Muscle Media 2000 put it, as "the steroid smuggler's worst nightmare."

Sole prosecutor.

18

United States v. David Jenkins, et al.

Counterfeit Steroids · Largest Ring in U.S. History

The Jenkins investigation unraveled the largest counterfeit steroid ring ever uncovered in the United States — a sophisticated operation producing and distributing fake anabolic steroids on a mass scale, exposing thousands of users to unknown and potentially dangerous substances in place of the drugs they believed they were purchasing. The ring operated through an elaborate distribution network that stretched across state lines, moving product through channels designed to mimic legitimate pharmaceutical supply chains.

The case built on a decade of accumulated expertise in steroid enforcement and represented the apex of Halpern's work in this area — the culmination of investigations that began with a single phone call from a border inspector in 1984 and ended with the dismantling of the most extensive counterfeit steroid operation the government had ever encountered. Jenkins and his co-conspirators were convicted on multiple counts.

19

United States v. William Cambra

Controlled Substances · First Federal HGH Prosecution

Human Growth Hormone had circulated for years in athletic and anti-aging circles — peddled as a performance enhancer, a recovery accelerant, and a so-called "fountain of youth" compound — before federal law caught up with its illegal distribution outside legitimate medical channels. The Cambra prosecution was the first federal case ever brought involving the illegal distribution of HGH, establishing the legal framework that would govern enforcement in this area for years to come.

The case signaled that the government's reach extended beyond traditional controlled substances to the full spectrum of performance-enhancing compounds being sold without prescription or medical supervision. Cambra's prosecution drew a line: HGH distributed outside legitimate medical channels was not a regulatory matter — it was a federal crime. The precedent proved consequential as HGH enforcement expanded in subsequent years across professional sports and the broader anti-aging industry.

Sole prosecutor.

20

United States v. Francis George Christie, et al.

Arms Smuggling · Presidential Embargo · First Federal Case

In the mid-1980s, President Reagan's executive order imposing a comprehensive trade embargo on Libya was the sharpest instrument in the U.S. foreign policy arsenal against Colonel Muammar Gaddafi's government. Christie and his co-conspirators sought to exploit it — moving prohibited goods to Libya through a network of intermediaries designed to conceal the ultimate destination of the transaction. The case became the first federal prosecution ever brought for violating the Presidential embargo of Libya.

Prosecuting an embargo violation required constructing a case that connected the defendants' conduct to the specific prohibitions of the executive order — novel legal territory at the time. The conviction demonstrated that the Presidential embargo carried real criminal teeth and that the Justice Department would pursue violators across the full complexity of international transactions designed to circumvent it. The case established precedent that subsequent embargo enforcement actions would draw upon.

Institution Building

Service & Leadership

Founder — Border Research & Technology Center

U.S. Department of Justice, Treasury & ONDCP · 1996

Halpern drafted the founding Memorandum of Understanding between the Department of Justice, the Department of the Treasury, and the Office of National Drug Control Policy that established the Border Research & Technology Center — a federal initiative designed to apply technology and interagency coordination to the challenges of border security enforcement. He subsequently played an active role in the creation of the Designated Commuter Lane at the San Ysidro Port of Entry and the development of the San Diego Border Tactical Wireless Interoperability Testbed, and was an early proponent of the NIJ Rapid Pursuit Task Force. Adjunct Professor of Law

University of San Diego School of Law · 1988–1995

For seven years while carrying a full federal caseload, Halpern taught upper-class and graduate law students on white-collar crime and corporate criminal responsibility — including theories of corporate liability, conflicts in multiple representation, and the outer limits of the attorney-client privilege in criminal investigations. He drew directly on active federal cases to teach what the law looks like when it meets the real world, and supervised law review articles on occasion.

Personal

From the Other Side

A Journal of Illness, Endurance, and What Remains

I spent four decades as a prosecutor—the last 36 of which were spent as an Assistant U.S. Attorney in the Southern District of California. It was a career built on gathering facts, following evidence, and reaching conclusions. The system, for all its many imperfections, was built on the idea that truth was immutable and answers were possible.

In the fall of 2020, I encountered a different kind of uncertainty. The kind that doesn’t resolve so cleanly. The kind that requires you to keep living inside the question, without any assurance it will ever be answered.

What follows is a journal I began after I was diagnosed with blood cancer—written for my family, my friends, and anyone else who finds themselves standing at a similar threshold. It is not a medical chronicle, though medicine is everywhere in it. It is an attempt to be honest about the reality of this experience: the fear, the clarity, the unexpected humor, the long stretches of waiting, and the strange gift of being forced to pay attention to your own life.

I am still writing it.

The story is not finished.

Journal Entries

Entry One

The Anomaly

The first clue was a number. It sat there—unremarkable—buried in a routine blood test. One of those annual checkups that feels more like paperwork than medicine. I didn’t feel sick. I wasn’t tired. Nothing hurt. If anything, I felt far better than men my age had any right to feel.

I was in my early 60s, working out every day—only on days ending in “y,” as I liked to tell anyone who showed any interest—and cycling more than 200 miles a week. My wife Marcia had retired from teaching middle school science and was busy working at the Museum of Natural History and walking the trails of San Diego County.

After hanging up my prosecutorial spurs, I was eagerly anticipating a second career writing op-eds for the local paper. In my spare time, I would race my bike, enjoy the occasional (well, make that regular) glass of something peaty, tackle various woodworking projects, and plan our next adventure.

Our three sons—Griffin, Moose, and Gator—were all grown and launched. Griffin was raising a family in San Sebastian, Spain, where he manufactured and sold kombucha (“Mucha Kombucha”). Moose was busy running a water sports business in Tahoe (“North Tahoe Wakesurf & Charter”). Gator had set up shop in the Bahamas and established Coral Vita—the world’s first land-based, commercial coral reef restoration business.

We had lived in our Mission Hills neighborhood for more than thirty years. It was, by any reasonable measure, a good moment to exhale.

But the number. The number in question—the one that started all of this—was my platelet count. In 2013, it was 280,000—comfortably within the normal range of 140,000 to 370,000. No one mentioned it. I didn’t ask. Life moved on.

But numbers, like facts in a case, have a way of accumulating quietly.

Year after year, mine crept upward. Not dramatically. Not enough to trigger alarm (especially for an endurance athlete whose body was continually being pushed to the limit). Just enough to be noticed—but only if someone was looking closely; and for a long time, no one was. By October 2020, it had reached 712,000. That is when the evidence began to accumulate in earnest.

Follow-up tests showed that my red and white blood cell counts were no longer quite right. Then came another result—one that didn’t just raise questions, but rewrote them entirely. A genetic mutation: JAK2 V617F. Not inherited. Acquired. The kind of change that appears over time, quietly, without permission—and without apparent cause.

It is commonly found in a family of diseases called myeloproliferative neoplasms (“MPNs”) where the bone marrow begins producing blood cells in ways it shouldn’t: too many, too few, or simply the wrong kind. My internist referred me to a hematologist.

Up to that point, the situation still felt abstract—numbers, terminology, a few raised eyebrows from doctors who spoke in measured tones and chose their words carefully. But somewhere between that referral and the first real conversation about what might be happening inside my bone marrow, the abstraction fell away.

Blood cancer.

It’s a broad term—almost deceptively so. There are more than 100 distinct subtypes, spread across categories like leukemia, lymphoma, myelodysplastic syndromes, and others I would soon learn far more about than I ever expected. Some are aggressive. Some move slowly. Some can be managed for years. Others cannot.

I had spent 40 years as a prosecutor—a career built on gathering facts, following evidence, and reaching conclusions. I was comfortable with complexity. I was comfortable with uncertainty, as long as it eventually resolved into an answer. This was different. I didn’t yet know where I would land on the spectrum of what was possible. But I had a sense—the kind that comes from long experience with how things unfold—that this was going to be less like a verdict and more like an opening statement.

The questions came quickly.

What exactly did I have? How serious was it? What were my options? And beneath all of that—though no one quite asked it out loud, not yet—how much time did I have?

I had spent a career chasing answers on behalf of others. Now I was the one waiting for them.

Entry Two

The Diagnosis (Possibly Flawed)

It was a warm, unremarkable morning in San Diego when I first went to see a hematologist. I put on my kit, hopped on my bike, and rode ten miles up the coast to UC San Diego’s Moores Cancer Center. The name meant nothing to me at the time—other than a passing association with a former owner of the San Diego Padres.

The complex I entered was part of a forest of sleek, metallic, glass-wrapped buildings that had sprouted up at UC San Diego over the last decade. Each bore the name of a benefactor—Sanford, Moores, Jacobs, Perlman, Koman. Philanthropy, and the vast currents of money flowing through modern medicine, have reshaped university campuses into something that feels like a cross between a research park and a glittering monument to our faith in progress.

From the outside, the message is unmistakable: precision, innovation, control. Inside the exam rooms, the reality proved more complicated. For all the investment, all the technology, all the brilliance brought to bear, diagnosis remained—at least in my case—an evolving hypothesis. The gleaming exterior suggested certainty. The lived experience felt closer to judgment—careful, informed, but still, inescapably, a blend of science and guesswork.

At the time, none of it registered. I rode into the lobby, walked my bike onto the elevator, and minutes later sat down in an exam room. It felt routine—another appointment, another data point. I did not yet understand that this place would become one of the central coordinates of the years ahead.

I still felt fine. That was the strange and disorienting part of all of this. No fatigue, no pain, no symptoms worth mentioning—nothing except an excess of platelets quietly multiplying in my bone marrow for what I would later learn had been, in all likelihood, the better part of a decade. The hematologist—Dr. Michael Choi, a blood cancer specialist with more than 20 years of experience—reviewed my results, considered my age, the JAK2 mutation, and the trajectory of my platelet count. He then carefully offered his working diagnosis: Essential Thrombocythemia, or ET, as it is known in the trade.

It was the first time anyone had tried to give a name to what was happening—and it had nothing to do with phoning home.

ET is a rare blood disorder in which the bone marrow produces too many platelets. Elevated platelet levels can lead to blood clots—thromboses—with serious consequences: strokes, heart attacks, pulmonary embolisms. The machinery of the body, in other words, begins to misfire.

There is also a paradox built into the disease that I found grimly clarifying. An ET patient can bleed excessively if a significant number of those excess platelets are malformed and unable to function properly. Too many platelets, and your blood clots when it shouldn’t. Too many defective ones, and it fails to clot when it should. The same condition that might kill you one way, under different circumstances, could kill you the other. Go figure.

Despite how ominous that sounds, ET is often manageable. Median survival runs approximately 20 years—more than 30 for patients diagnosed before 60. Based on my risk profile, Dr. Choi considered me at elevated risk for clotting. His prescription: two daily low-dose aspirin tablets and a single hydroxyurea pill, a form of chemotherapy used to slow the overproduction of blood cells.

At first, I didn’t question either the prognosis or the treatment. The case appeared straightforward. The sentence, extremely palatable. What was there to question?

My subsequent reading—undertaken with the same instinct that led me to interview every witness on multiple occasions—confirmed that hydroxyurea was the standard first-line treatment for ET. The weight of medical opinion tipped the scale entirely in one direction. I accepted the diagnosis and started the medication.

But something kept nagging at me.

What I didn’t know at the time—what I would only learn later, after doing what I always did when the initial evidence didn’t add up—was that Dr. Choi, by his own candid admission, had seen perhaps six to ten ET patients in his entire career. He was a good doctor. Patient, kind, humane. He was working from the available evidence and the standard literature. But less than a dozen cases is not a large sample on which to base a diagnosis of a rare disease—particularly one with multiple presentations and significant treatment consequences.

Before I left that first appointment, Dr. Choi scheduled a bone marrow biopsy.

The purpose of the biopsy was to determine whether I might be dealing with something more serious than ET—a condition called myelofibrosis, in which scar tissue gradually replaces the bone marrow’s ability to produce healthy blood cells. He mentioned it matter-of-factly, the way doctors mention things they don’t want to alarm you about. I noted his tone. I had used the same one myself, in other rooms, delivering other kinds of news.

I went home and looked it up.
